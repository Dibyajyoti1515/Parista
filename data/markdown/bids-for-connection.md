# Bids for Connection (Gottman)

Source: John Gottman (research beginning in the 1990s, based on observational studies of couples)

A bid for connection is any small attempt one person makes to get attention, affirmation, affection, or support from another person. It can be as tiny as pointing out an interesting cloud, asking "how was your day?", sending a funny meme, or sighing loudly after a hard meeting. On its own, a single bid looks trivial. What matters is the pattern: over time, the way people respond to each other's bids — by turning toward them, turning away, or turning against them — becomes one of the clearest predictors of how connected a relationship feels.

Turning toward means responding with interest or warmth, even briefly — a glance up from a phone, a "tell me more," a quick laugh. Turning away means missing the bid entirely, often not out of malice but distraction or fatigue. Turning against means responding with irritation, dismissiveness, or sarcasm. None of these responses need to be dramatic to matter. It's the accumulation of thousands of small moments, not one big event, that shapes whether people in a relationship feel like they can reliably reach each other.

This idea applies well beyond romantic partnerships. Family members make bids at the dinner table. Coworkers make bids in a Slack message or a hallway comment. Recognizing a bid for what it is — a small request to be noticed — can change how a person interprets what might otherwise seem like a random or unimportant remark.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Someone shares a small piece of news or observation, seemingly out of nowhere ("this coffee is really good today")
- A person asks a question that could be answered in one word but seems to be inviting more conversation
- Silence or a change of subject follows what felt like an attempt to connect, leaving the person who reached out looking briefly deflated
- One person repeatedly initiates small check-ins (texts, comments, questions) while the other rarely reciprocates
- A comment delivered with unusual energy or emotion, even if the content itself seems minor, as if testing whether the other person is paying attention

## Why this matters for conflict

Many conflicts that seem to be "about" a specific disagreement are actually fueled by a long buildup of missed bids. When bids go unanswered often enough, the person making them tends to stop trying, and the relationship can start to feel distant or tense without either person being able to name exactly why. In family and workplace settings, this can look like someone becoming quieter, more irritable, or more prone to overreacting to small things — often because the small things have become stand-ins for a much larger sense of not being noticed.

Understanding bids for connection gives people a concrete, low-stakes way to repair or strengthen a relationship: instead of trying to resolve the "big" issue directly, they can start by noticing and responding to the small bids happening in daily interactions. It also reframes conflict itself — a sharp comment or a withdrawal is sometimes easier to understand once it's seen as a reaction to bids that were missed, rather than as an attack that needs to be defended against. This shift in framing can help people approach a disagreement with curiosity about what's underneath it, rather than jumping straight to who's right.
