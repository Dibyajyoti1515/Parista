# Passive-Aggressive Communication Patterns

Source: The term originated in a 1945 U.S. military technical bulletin describing soldiers' indirect resistance to orders; later expanded into broader psychological and communication research (1960s onward)

Passive-aggressive communication describes a pattern where negative feelings — frustration, resentment, disagreement — get expressed indirectly rather than stated openly. Instead of saying "I'm upset that you didn't help with this," a passive-aggressive pattern might look like sarcasm, procrastinating on a task out of quiet resentment, giving one-word answers while insisting nothing is wrong, or making a pointed comment disguised as a joke. The term originated in a military context describing soldiers who resisted orders indirectly rather than refusing outright, and it was later broadened to describe a similar dynamic in everyday relationships: opposition or anger expressed through indirect action rather than direct statement.

This pattern often develops as a response to environments where expressing anger or disagreement directly felt unsafe, unwelcome, or ineffective — a family where conflict was discouraged, a workplace where pushing back on a superior felt risky, or a relationship where direct confrontation historically led to a worse outcome than staying quiet. In that light, passive-aggressive behavior can be understood less as manipulation and more as an indirect strategy for expressing something that felt too risky to say plainly. That said, understanding its origin doesn't make it easy for the person on the receiving end, who is often left confused, since the message being communicated (frustration, disagreement) doesn't match the words being used.

The defining feature of this pattern is the mismatch between what's said and what's actually meant, which creates a particular kind of conflict: the person expressing frustration indirectly often feels their point has been made, while the person receiving it may not even register that a conflict is happening, or may sense something is wrong without being able to name it. This mismatch tends to prevent the actual issue from ever being addressed directly, since the surface interaction (a terse reply, a canceled plan, a sarcastic comment) rarely opens into the real conversation underneath it.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Saying "I'm fine" or "it's nothing" while behavior clearly signals otherwise — withdrawal, coldness, or terseness
- Expressing criticism through sarcasm, jokes, or backhanded comments rather than direct statements
- Agreeing to a request outwardly while delaying, doing it poorly, or "forgetting" as an indirect form of resistance
- Bringing up old grievances during unrelated moments, rather than addressing them when they originally happened
- A pattern of one person feeling confused or blindsided by another's anger, because it was never stated directly before it showed up indirectly

## Why this matters for conflict

Passive-aggressive patterns show up across all three domains — romantic relationships, family dynamics, and workplace interactions — often as a way of avoiding the discomfort of direct confrontation while still registering disagreement somehow. In romantic relationships, this can look like the silent treatment or subtly punishing behavior after an unaddressed disagreement. In families, it can look like a relative who agrees to something at a gathering, then complains about it to someone else instead of the person directly. In workplaces, it can look like an employee who agrees to a deadline they have no intention of meeting, or a colleague who copies a manager on emails as a way of registering a complaint indirectly rather than raising it in conversation.

Recognizing this pattern — in oneself or others — matters because it points toward a specific, addressable gap: somewhere along the way, direct expression of disagreement or frustration came to feel unsafe or ineffective. Rather than reacting only to the surface behavior (the sarcasm, the delay, the coldness), it can be more productive to gently name what seems to be happening underneath it ("it seems like something's bothering you — I'd rather hear it directly") and to make clear that direct expression will actually be received well. For someone who recognizes this pattern in their own behavior, the more direct path forward is practicing stating frustration plainly, even in small ways, rather than relying on indirect signals that the other person may never accurately decode.
