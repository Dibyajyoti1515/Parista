# Cognitive Distortions

Source: Beck (1976)

Cognitive distortions are habitual patterns of thinking that skew how a
situation is interpreted, often making it feel worse or more threatening
than the facts alone would suggest. They're especially common during
emotionally charged conflicts, when it's easy to reach for the most
alarming interpretation of an ambiguous event. A few distortions are
particularly relevant to relationship misunderstandings.

## Mind-reading

Assuming you know what someone else is thinking or feeling without them
telling you — for example, assuming a partner's silence means they're
angry, when they might simply be busy or processing their own thoughts.

## Catastrophizing

Jumping to the worst-case interpretation of an event. A single tense
conversation becomes "this relationship is falling apart," rather than
being treated as one difficult moment among many.

## Personalization

Assuming that someone else's mood, behavior, or decision is about you, when
it may have little or nothing to do with you at all. A partner's bad mood
after a hard day at work can get misread as being upset with you
specifically.

## Black-and-white thinking

Viewing a situation in absolute terms — "they never listen to me," "we
always fight" — when the reality is usually more mixed. This kind of
language tends to escalate conflict because it erases the exceptions that
would otherwise soften the complaint.

## Why this matters for conflict

A large share of relationship misunderstandings come not from what
actually happened, but from the interpretation layered on top of it.
Recognizing when a reaction is being shaped by one of these distortions
doesn't mean the underlying feeling is invalid — it means it's worth
separating the feeling from the specific interpretation that triggered it,
since the interpretation is often the more fixable part.
