# Confirmation Bias in Conflict

Source: Rooted in Peter Wason's cognitive psychology research (1960s), later applied extensively to social and relationship contexts by subsequent researchers (1970s–present)

Confirmation bias describes the tendency to notice, remember, and give more weight to information that supports what someone already believes, while overlooking or discounting information that contradicts it. Psychologist Peter Wason first demonstrated this tendency through experiments on logical reasoning, finding that people consistently sought out evidence that confirmed their existing assumptions rather than testing them against contrary evidence. This tendency turned out to apply far beyond logic puzzles — it shapes how people interpret nearly everything, including the people closest to them.

In conflict specifically, confirmation bias tends to operate through a kind of selective memory and selective attention. Once someone forms a belief about another person — "they're inconsiderate," "they don't respect me," "they're not trying" — everyday interactions get filtered through that belief. Moments that fit the belief tend to be noticed and remembered vividly, while moments that contradict it tend to be minimized, forgotten, or explained away as exceptions rather than counter-evidence. A partner who forms the belief that their spouse doesn't help enough around the house may vividly remember every time the spouse didn't help, while barely registering the times they did, treating those moments as flukes rather than evidence the original belief might be incomplete.

This creates a self-reinforcing loop that's often invisible to the person experiencing it, because it doesn't feel like bias — it feels like accurately noticing a real pattern. The evidence being gathered feels objective, since it's genuinely happening; what's missed is that a roughly equal amount of contradicting evidence may also be happening and simply not being weighted the same way. Over time, this can harden into a fixed narrative about someone — a coworker who's "always difficult," a family member who "never listens" — that becomes increasingly resistant to updating, even as new, contradicting behavior occurs.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Remembering specific instances that confirm a negative belief about someone in vivid detail, while struggling to recall contradicting examples
- Interpreting ambiguous behavior in a way that consistently fits an existing narrative about a person, rather than considering other explanations
- Dismissing positive or neutral behavior from someone as an exception, a fluke, or "not really counting," while treating negative behavior as representative
- Feeling like a belief about someone ("they never," "they always") is obviously true, without having actually tracked behavior over time
- Noticing that a friend or outside observer sees a person's behavior more evenhandedly than one's own account of it suggests

## Why this matters for conflict

Confirmation bias plays a significant role in conflicts that feel stuck or repetitive, because both people may be unconsciously collecting evidence that confirms their existing view of the other, while filtering out evidence that might complicate it. In romantic relationships, this can entrench a narrative ("they don't care as much as I do") that becomes harder to challenge over time, even as the partner's actual behavior varies. In family conflict, a long-held view of a relative ("the difficult one," "the one who never changes") can persist for years, filtering out any behavior that doesn't fit the label. In workplace conflict, a manager or colleague who's formed an early negative impression of someone may keep noticing behavior that confirms it while overlooking genuine improvement or contradicting effort.

Recognizing this pattern is useful because it opens the possibility of deliberately checking a belief against more complete evidence, rather than relying on memory alone, which is naturally skewed toward whatever confirms existing assumptions. This might mean consciously trying to recall counter-examples before concluding a pattern is fixed, or asking a neutral third party how they perceive the situation, since outside observers aren't carrying the same accumulated bias. It also means treating strong, longstanding conclusions about another person's character with some healthy skepticism — not because the underlying concern is necessarily wrong, but because the evidence supporting it may be less complete and less balanced than it feels.
