# Power Distance in Manager-Employee Conflict

Source: Geert Hofstede (cross-cultural research, developed through the 1970s–1980s)

Power distance is a concept developed by researcher Geert Hofstede to describe how much a given culture, organization, or relationship accepts and expects unequal distribution of power. In workplaces, this shows up as the degree to which employees feel comfortable questioning, disagreeing with, or giving feedback to those above them in the hierarchy. In a high power-distance environment, authority is expected to go largely unquestioned — employees defer to managers, disagreement is expressed indirectly if at all, and raising concerns upward feels risky or inappropriate. In a low power-distance environment, hierarchy still exists, but there's a cultural expectation that employees can speak candidly, challenge decisions, and expect managers to justify their choices without that being seen as insubordination.

While Hofstede originally studied this as a difference between national cultures, the same dynamic plays out at a smaller scale between individual managers and employees, and between different organizations even within the same country. A manager who assumes a low power-distance style — expecting open pushback and treating silence as agreement — can badly misread an employee who was raised or trained in a higher power-distance environment, where staying quiet, deferring, or not raising concerns directly is the expected, respectful way to behave. Conversely, an employee accustomed to higher power distance may perceive a manager's request for direct feedback as a trap or an inappropriate overstep, even when the manager genuinely wants honest input.

This mismatch creates a particular kind of conflict that's easy to misdiagnose. A manager might conclude an employee is disengaged, lacks initiative, or is hiding problems, when the employee is actually behaving exactly as their sense of appropriate workplace conduct dictates. An employee might conclude a manager is authoritarian or uninterested in their opinion, when the manager is genuinely waiting for input that isn't coming because the employee doesn't feel it's their place to offer it unprompted. Both people can be acting in good faith while completely misreading the other's intentions.

## Recognizing the pattern

- An employee rarely raises concerns, disagreements, or problems directly, even when something is clearly going wrong
- A manager interprets silence in meetings as agreement or lack of concerns, when it may actually reflect discomfort with speaking up
- Feedback, when it happens, tends to flow only downward (manager to employee) rather than in both directions
- An employee expresses concerns indirectly — through a third party, in private, or through subtle behavioral cues — rather than raising them openly
- A manager's attempt to "flatten" the hierarchy (open-door policy, casual tone, inviting pushback) doesn't actually produce more candid input from employees

## Why this matters for conflict

In workplace and HR conflict, power distance mismatches often get misread as performance or attitude problems rather than what they usually are: differing, often deeply ingrained assumptions about how hierarchy is supposed to work. An employee who doesn't flag a problem early isn't necessarily being careless or dishonest — they may be operating under a real belief that raising concerns unprompted isn't their place. A manager who seems to dominate meetings isn't necessarily uninterested in employee input — they may be operating in an environment where that level of directness from leadership is simply expected and normal.

Recognizing this pattern helps reframe workplace conflict resolution: rather than assuming an employee's silence means agreement, or a manager's directness means disinterest in feedback, it becomes useful to explicitly discuss expectations around communication and hierarchy rather than assuming they're shared. In practice, this might mean a manager creating structured, low-risk ways for employees to raise concerns (written feedback channels, anonymous input, one-on-one check-ins) rather than relying on employees to speak up unprompted in group settings — and employees being told clearly and repeatedly that direct input is genuinely wanted, since a single invitation is often not enough to overcome a deeply held expectation about appropriate workplace conduct.
