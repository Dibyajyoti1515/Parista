# The Five Stages of Grief and Loss

Source: Kübler-Ross & Kessler (2005)

Originally developed to describe the emotional experience of terminally
ill patients, the five-stage framework has since been widely applied to
other forms of loss, including the end of a significant relationship. The
five stages are denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance.

## Denial

A protective response that softens the initial shock of a loss — difficulty
believing the relationship is actually over, or a sense of numbness that
delays the full emotional impact.

## Anger

As the reality of the loss sets in, anger often surfaces — at the other
person, at oneself, at the situation, or at nothing in particular. Anger
can feel more bearable than the sadness underneath it, which is part of
why it often appears early.

## Bargaining

An attempt to regain a sense of control over the loss — replaying what
could have been done differently, imagining scenarios where the
relationship could be salvaged, or making internal promises about what
would change if given another chance.

## Depression

A period of sadness as the full weight of the loss is acknowledged. This
stage is often mistaken for something is going wrong, when it's actually a
natural and necessary part of processing a real loss.

## Acceptance

Not the same as being "over it" or approving of what happened — acceptance
means acknowledging the loss as real and beginning to build a life that
accounts for it.

## Why this matters

These stages are not a strict sequence — people can move between them,
skip some, or revisit earlier stages unexpectedly. Someone processing a
breakup may not be doing anything wrong if their experience doesn't match
this order exactly. The framework is most useful as a way to recognize
that intense, seemingly contradictory emotions (anger one day, deep sadness
the next) are a normal part of processing a real loss, not a sign that
something is uniquely wrong with how a person is coping.
