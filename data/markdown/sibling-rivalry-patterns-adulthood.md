# Sibling Rivalry Patterns in Adulthood

Source: Rooted in Alfred Adler's theory of birth order and family constellation (early 1900s–1930s), later expanded by contemporary sibling relationship researchers including Victor Cicirelli (1980s–1990s)

Alfred Adler was among the first to argue that a person's place within their family — how they compared to siblings for attention, achievement, and parental approval — shaped their personality and behavior well beyond childhood. He theorized that siblings often unconsciously compete for a limited sense of parental attention and approval, developing distinct roles (the responsible one, the rebellious one, the achiever, the peacekeeper) partly in response to each other rather than purely as individual traits. These roles, formed early, often persist far longer than most people expect, resurfacing in adult interactions long after the original competition for parental attention has technically ended.

Later researchers, including Victor Cicirelli, studied how these early dynamics carry into adult sibling relationships, finding that patterns established in childhood — who was seen as more responsible, more favored, or more capable — tend to resurface during major family events: caregiving for aging parents, managing an inheritance, planning a wedding or funeral, or even routine gatherings like holidays. The specific issue at hand (who's handling a parent's medical care, how an estate gets divided, who hosts Thanksgiving) often becomes a stage for old roles and old comparisons to replay, even among adults who otherwise get along and have built independent, successful lives.

What makes adult sibling rivalry distinct from other family conflict is this layering of past and present: the disagreement in the moment is real, but it's often carrying the weight of decades-old dynamics — a sense of who was always favored, who always had to be the responsible one, who never got taken seriously. Because these roles were often set before either sibling had much say in them, adult siblings can find themselves reacting to a present-day disagreement with an intensity that seems to come from somewhere deeper than the immediate issue.

## Recognizing the pattern

- A disagreement about a practical matter (caregiving, finances, planning) escalates well beyond what the specific issue seems to warrant
- One sibling consistently falls into a familiar role — the responsible one, the one who gives in, the one who gets blamed — regardless of the actual situation
- Comparisons to childhood treatment surface during adult disagreements ("you were always the favorite," "I always had to be the one who handled things")
- Rivalry resurfaces specifically around major family events or transitions, like a parent's illness, a wedding, or a death, even between siblings who rarely conflict otherwise
- A sibling relationship feels stuck in old dynamics despite both people having changed significantly as adults

## Why this matters for conflict

Recognizing that adult sibling conflict often carries old family roles helps explain why certain disagreements feel disproportionately charged compared to their actual stakes. A dispute over who's doing more for an aging parent, for example, is rarely just about logistics — it often reactivates a much older question about fairness, favoritism, and being seen, dating back to childhood. Naming this layering can help siblings separate the present issue from the historical pattern, making room to address the practical matter directly instead of relitigating decades of family dynamics through it.

This understanding also opens the door to a different kind of conversation — one where siblings can acknowledge the old pattern explicitly ("I think we're falling into our old roles here") rather than assuming the current conflict reflects who the other person has become as an adult. This is particularly valuable during high-stakes family transitions like caregiving and inheritance, where old sibling roles are most likely to resurface under pressure, and where recognizing the pattern early can prevent a practical disagreement from reopening much older wounds.
