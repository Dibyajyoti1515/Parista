# Anxious-Avoidant Trap (attachment mismatch dynamics)

Source: Rooted in attachment theory, originally developed by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth (1960s-70s), later popularized for adult relationships by researchers like Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1980s) and clinicians such as Amir Levine and Rachel Heller (2000s).

Some people cope with closeness by leaning in harder when they feel uncertain about a relationship — checking in more, seeking reassurance, wanting to talk things through immediately. Others cope with closeness by pulling back — needing space, going quiet, feeling smothered when a partner or family member wants more contact than they do. Neither style is a flaw; both are ways people learned, often early in life, to manage their need for connection alongside their need for safety and independence.

The trap happens when these two styles pair up. One person's bid for reassurance can feel like pressure to the other, so they withdraw. That withdrawal then feels like confirmation of the first person's fear of being abandoned, so they reach out even harder. The harder reaching triggers more withdrawal. Round and round it goes — not because either person is doing something wrong, but because each one's coping strategy accidentally triggers the other's deepest worry.

What makes this pattern so sticky is that both people are usually acting in good faith. The one seeking closeness genuinely just wants to feel secure. The one pulling back genuinely just wants room to breathe. Without a name for the pattern, it's easy for each side to conclude the other doesn't care, is too needy, or is emotionally unavailable — when really, they're both just running an old script under stress.

## Recognizing the pattern

- One person tends to initiate contact, check in, or ask "are we okay?" more often during periods of tension
- The other tends to go quiet, delay responding, or change the subject when things feel emotionally intense
- Small disagreements escalate quickly because pursuit and withdrawal feed each other
- The pursuing person often feels increasingly anxious or rejected; the withdrawing person often feels increasingly overwhelmed or trapped
- After a blowup or a cooldown, both people can usually agree they didn't want things to go that far

## Why this matters for conflict

In romantic relationships, family dynamics, and even workplace disagreements, this push-pull pattern is one of the most common reasons the same fight seems to happen over and over in slightly different clothing. A parent who calls repeatedly and an adult child who stops answering, a manager who follows up constantly and an employee who goes silent under pressure, a partner who wants to "talk about us" and another who needs a night to think — these are often the same underlying mechanic showing up in different settings.

Understanding this pattern helps people stop taking the other person's coping style as a personal verdict on the relationship. The person seeking reassurance can learn to name what they need directly instead of escalating contact, and the person seeking space can learn to signal "I'm still here, I just need a minute" instead of going fully silent. Neither move erases the underlying difference in style, but naming the cycle out loud — "I think we're doing the thing where I chase and you retreat" — often does more to defuse a conflict than trying to resolve whatever the argument was ostensibly about.
