# Cognitive Reappraisal (reframing)

Source: James Gross (emotion regulation research, beginning in the mid-1990s, Stanford University)

Cognitive reappraisal refers to the process of changing how someone interprets a situation in order to change the emotional response to it, without changing the situation itself. Psychologist James Gross, whose research shaped much of the modern understanding of emotion regulation, studied reappraisal as one of several strategies people use to manage their emotional reactions, and found it to be one of the more effective ones — more effective, in many cases, than trying to suppress an emotion once it's already been triggered. The core idea is straightforward: emotions aren't a direct, fixed response to events themselves, but a response to how those events are interpreted, which means changing the interpretation can genuinely change the emotional experience, not just how it's expressed outwardly.

A common example involves interpreting the same behavior in two different ways. If a partner cancels plans last minute, one interpretation might be "they don't prioritize me," which tends to produce hurt or anger. Another interpretation of the identical event might be "they're overwhelmed and struggling to manage everything right now," which tends to produce concern or patience instead. The event hasn't changed, but the emotional response shifts significantly depending on which interpretation feels most plausible. Gross's research distinguished this from emotional suppression, which involves masking or holding back an emotional reaction without addressing the underlying interpretation — a strategy his studies found to be less effective and more mentally taxing over time, since the original emotional response tends to remain active even when it isn't outwardly expressed.

Reappraisal isn't about talking oneself into a false or overly rosy interpretation of every situation — some interpretations genuinely are accurate, and some situations genuinely call for concern or anger. The skill lies in noticing when an initial interpretation is one of several plausible readings of ambiguous events, rather than the only possible one, and consciously considering whether a different, equally reasonable interpretation might be available. This is especially relevant in conflict, where the same ambiguous behavior — a delayed reply, a short tone, a canceled plan — can often be read multiple ways, and the interpretation chosen tends to shape both the emotional reaction and what happens next in the conflict.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Reacting strongly to an ambiguous event (a short text, a delayed response, a canceled plan) based on one particular interpretation, without considering alternatives
- Noticing that the same type of situation produces very different emotional reactions depending on mood, context, or who's involved
- Feeling calmer or less reactive after deliberately considering another explanation for someone's behavior, even without confirming it's true
- A pattern of assuming the most negative available interpretation of ambiguous situations by default
- Difficulty separating "what happened" from "what I've decided it means," treating an interpretation as though it were an established fact

## Why this matters for conflict

In romantic relationships, family conflict, and workplace disagreements, a significant amount of unnecessary escalation comes from reacting to an interpretation of events rather than the events themselves — and interpretations, especially under stress, tend to default toward the most negative plausible explanation. A colleague's terse email might be read as disrespect, when it might just as easily reflect a stressful morning. A family member's missed call might be read as avoidance, when it might simply reflect a busy day. Recognizing that an emotional reaction is often responding to an interpretation, not a certainty, opens space to pause and consider whether that specific interpretation is the only reasonable one available.

This matters practically because reappraisal, unlike suppression, actually changes the felt emotional experience rather than just masking it — which means it tends to reduce the intensity of a reaction before it turns into an accusation or an escalated conflict, rather than requiring someone to manage an emotion they're still fully feeling while trying to act calm. In practice, this might look like pausing before reacting to an ambiguous message and asking "what's another explanation for this that's just as plausible," or directly asking the other person for their intended meaning rather than assuming the most negative one. This doesn't mean ignoring real problems — it means making sure the emotional reaction is responding to what's actually happening, not just to the first interpretation that came to mind.
