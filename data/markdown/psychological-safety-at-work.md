# Psychological Safety at Work

Source: Amy Edmondson (research beginning in the mid-1990s, Harvard Business School)

Psychological safety refers to a shared belief within a team that it's safe to take interpersonal risks — asking a question that might seem obvious, admitting a mistake, raising a concern, proposing an unusual idea, or disagreeing with someone more senior — without fear of embarrassment, punishment, or damage to how one is seen. Researcher Amy Edmondson, who coined the term through her studies of hospital teams and later broader workplace research, found that psychological safety wasn't the same as being liked or having a comfortable, low-conflict environment. It specifically describes whether people believe that speaking up, especially about problems or uncertainty, will be met with a reasonable response rather than blame or ridicule.

Edmondson's early research produced a somewhat counterintuitive finding: hospital teams that reported more errors weren't necessarily making more mistakes than other teams — they often had higher psychological safety, which meant people felt safe admitting and reporting mistakes rather than hiding them. Teams with lower psychological safety often had similar or higher actual error rates, but fewer errors got reported or caught early, because admitting a mistake felt too risky. This distinction matters enormously for conflict: psychological safety isn't about avoiding disagreement or friction, it's about whether people can surface problems, disagreements, and mistakes honestly, which is often what prevents small issues from becoming larger conflicts later.

Low psychological safety tends to produce a particular set of behaviors that can look like other problems from the outside: people staying quiet in meetings, concerns getting raised only privately or after the fact, mistakes getting hidden or minimized until they're unavoidable, and a general reluctance to challenge decisions even when something seems clearly off. These behaviors are often mistaken for disengagement or poor judgment, when they're frequently a rational response to an environment where speaking up has previously carried real social or professional cost.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Team members rarely ask questions or admit confusion in group settings, even on complex or ambiguous topics
- Mistakes or problems tend to surface late, after they've become harder to fix, rather than being flagged early
- Disagreement with a decision, especially one made by someone senior, is expressed privately (if at all) rather than in the room where the decision is being made
- One or two people dominate discussion while others stay notably quiet, even on topics within their expertise
- Feedback about a mistake or problem is met with defensiveness or blame, which tends to reduce future reporting of similar issues

## Why this matters for conflict

Low psychological safety is often at the root of workplace conflicts that seem to appear suddenly but were actually building for a long time — a project that fails because concerns were never raised, a conflict between colleagues that escalates because neither felt safe addressing it directly and early, or an HR issue that surfaces only after it's become serious because reporting it earlier felt too risky. Recognizing psychological safety as a factor reframes these situations: the problem often isn't that people didn't notice issues, but that the environment didn't make it safe to name them when they were still small.

Understanding this concept is useful for both managers and employees navigating workplace conflict. For managers, it highlights that responses to mistakes and disagreement — even subtle ones, like a sharp tone or visible frustration — get closely watched and remembered, shaping whether people bring future problems forward or bury them. For employees, it offers language for something that might otherwise feel like a vague, hard-to-articulate discomfort: recognizing that hesitation to speak up isn't necessarily a personal shortcoming, but often a reasonable read of an environment where speaking up hasn't been safe. Rebuilding psychological safety after it's been damaged usually requires consistent, visible evidence that raising problems is genuinely welcomed — not just stated as a value.
