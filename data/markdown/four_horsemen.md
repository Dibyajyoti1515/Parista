# The Four Horsemen of Conflict

Source: Gottman & Levenson (1992)

Through decades of observing couples in conflict, researchers identified
four communication patterns that reliably predict relationship breakdown
when they become habitual. These patterns are commonly referred to as the
Four Horsemen.

## 1. Criticism

Attacking a partner's character rather than addressing a specific behavior.
The difference between a complaint ("I was upset you were late") and
criticism ("You're always so inconsiderate") is that criticism generalizes
a specific event into a statement about who the person fundamentally is.

## 2. Contempt

Communicating disgust, superiority, or disrespect — through sarcasm,
mockery, eye-rolling, or name-calling. Contempt is considered the most
corrosive of the four, because it signals a lack of basic respect for the
other person rather than disagreement with a specific action.

## 3. Defensiveness

Responding to a concern by deflecting blame or playing the victim, rather
than acknowledging any part of the complaint. Defensiveness often escalates
conflict because it signals to the other person that their concern isn't
being heard at all.

## 4. Stonewalling

Withdrawing from the interaction entirely — shutting down, going silent,
or physically leaving — usually as a response to feeling overwhelmed.
Stonewalling often follows the other three patterns once a conflict has
escalated past a point either partner can productively engage with.

## Why this framework matters

None of these patterns appearing occasionally is necessarily a sign of a
doomed relationship — the research is about *habitual* patterns, not single
incidents. What matters is whether a conflict pattern repeats over time
without repair attempts (moments where either partner tries to de-escalate
or reconnect). Identifying which of the four horsemen is present in a
specific conflict can help name what's actually happening beneath the
surface content of an argument.
