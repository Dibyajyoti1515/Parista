# Feedback Sandwich vs Direct Feedback (why the sandwich fails)

Source: The feedback sandwich technique has unclear origins in management training literature (popularized from the 1980s onward); critiques draw on organizational psychology research, including work by Kim Scott on "radical candor" (2010s)

The feedback sandwich is a widely taught technique for delivering criticism: open with a genuine compliment, deliver the critical feedback in the middle, then close with another positive comment, so the difficult message is "cushioned" on both sides. The intention behind it is understandable — criticism is uncomfortable to give and receive, and softening it seems like a reasonable way to protect the relationship while still communicating something important.

In practice, though, the technique tends to undermine the very thing it's trying to accomplish. Once someone has experienced the pattern a few times, they often start listening for the criticism buried in the middle and discount the praise on either side as setup, rather than genuine feedback. This means the positive comments lose their value as authentic recognition, while the critical comment can end up feeling more concerning specifically because of how it was wrapped, since the softening starts to be interpreted as a signal that something serious is being downplayed. There's also a more direct problem: sandwiching often makes the actual message less clear. A specific concern (a missed deadline, an unclear pattern in someone's work) can get diluted enough between two positive comments that the recipient walks away unsure exactly what needs to change.

Organizational researchers, including Kim Scott's work on what she termed "radical candor," have argued for an alternative approach that combines direct honesty with genuine personal care, rather than trying to disguise honesty as something more palatable. This approach separates two things that the sandwich technique often conflates: caring about someone, and softening what's said to them. It's possible to be direct and specific about a problem while still communicating real respect and investment in the person's success — in fact, vague or indirect feedback is often experienced as less caring, not more, because it deprives someone of the clear information they need to actually improve.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Feedback that starts with praise, shifts to criticism, and ends with more praise, following a predictable and often transparent structure
- The recipient seems to brace or wait for the "but" as soon as positive feedback begins, rather than receiving it as genuine
- After feedback, the recipient is unclear about what specifically needs to change, despite feedback technically having been given
- Praise from a particular manager or colleague starts to feel less meaningful over time, as though it's routinely a setup for criticism
- A pattern of avoiding direct statements about a problem, instead hinting at it indirectly or burying it in qualifications

## Why this matters for conflict

In workplace and HR conflict specifically, unclear or overly cushioned feedback is a common source of long-running tension, because the person receiving feedback often doesn't fully register that a real problem exists until it's escalated to something more serious, like a formal warning or a difficult performance conversation. If early feedback was delivered so gently that it didn't register as urgent, the eventual serious conversation can feel unfair or sudden to the recipient, even though, from the manager's perspective, the issue had technically been raised before.

Understanding why the sandwich approach often backfires supports a more effective alternative: separating clarity from kindness, rather than trying to blend them into one softened message. This means stating the specific issue plainly and early, paired with clear care and support for improvement, rather than hoping a wrapped message will land gently. This shift matters beyond formal performance reviews too — in day-to-day workplace conflict, and even in personal relationships, feedback that's both kind and clear tends to prevent much larger, harder conversations later, while feedback that's kind but vague often just delays a problem rather than resolving it.
