# Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

Source: Rosenberg (2003)

Nonviolent Communication is a framework for expressing needs and feelings
in a way that reduces defensiveness and increases the chance of being
genuinely heard. It structures a difficult conversation around four
components.

## 1. Observation

Stating what actually happened, without evaluation or judgment mixed in.
"You didn't reply for six hours" is an observation. "You ignored me" is an
interpretation layered on top of the observation.

## 2. Feeling

Naming the emotional reaction to the observation, using a feeling word
rather than a thought disguised as a feeling. "I felt anxious" is a
feeling. "I felt like you didn't care" is actually a judgment about the
other person's intent, not a feeling.

## 3. Need

Identifying the underlying need connected to the feeling — for example,
a need for reassurance, connection, respect, or predictability. This is
often the part people skip, going straight from feeling to blame instead
of naming what they actually need.

## 4. Request

Making a specific, actionable request rather than a vague demand or an
implicit expectation. "Could you send a quick text if you're going to be
unreachable for a while?" is a request. "You need to be more considerate"
is not — it's too vague to act on.

## Why this framework matters for conflict

Much of what escalates a disagreement isn't the underlying need itself —
it's how the concern gets communicated. Leading with judgment ("you always
do this") tends to trigger defensiveness, which shuts down the actual
conversation about the need underneath. Restructuring a complaint into
observation → feeling → need → request doesn't guarantee resolution, but it
gives the other person something concrete to respond to instead of
something to defend against.
