# Family Systems Theory (Bowen — triangulation, differentiation)

Source: Murray Bowen (developed through clinical work and research from the 1950s–1970s)

Murray Bowen, a psychiatrist who studied family patterns for decades, proposed that families function less like a collection of separate individuals and more like a single emotional system, where each person's thoughts, feelings, and actions are connected to and influenced by everyone else in the system. A change in one family member's behavior tends to ripple outward, affecting how others feel and act, often without anyone realizing the connection. Two of Bowen's most widely used ideas — differentiation and triangulation — describe how this system tends to function under stress.

Differentiation refers to a person's ability to maintain their own sense of identity, beliefs, and emotional stability while staying closely connected to family members, especially during conflict or tension. Someone with higher differentiation can disagree with a parent or sibling without either losing themselves in the relationship or cutting off from it entirely — they can stay connected while still thinking and feeling independently. Someone with lower differentiation tends to either fuse with the family's emotional climate (absorbing others' anxiety, opinions, or moods as their own) or react by cutting off distance entirely to escape that fusion. Bowen saw differentiation less as a fixed trait and more as a capacity that varies by relationship and can shift over a lifetime.

Triangulation describes what happens when tension between two family members gets stabilized by pulling in a third person, rather than being addressed directly between the original two. This might look like a parent venting about a spouse to a child, two siblings each confiding in a third about the other, or a family member consistently relaying messages between two others who aren't speaking directly. Triangles form because two-person relationships under stress are often unstable — bringing in a third person can temporarily reduce the tension between the original two, but it does so by redistributing the anxiety rather than resolving it, and it often puts the third person in an uncomfortable, loyalty-straining position.

## Recognizing the pattern

- A family member routinely discusses one relative's problems or shortcomings with another relative, rather than addressing them directly
- Feeling caught in the middle, expected to relay messages or take sides between two other family members who aren't speaking directly
- Noticing a shift in mood or opinion that seems to mirror a parent's or sibling's anxiety, rather than reflecting one's own independent view
- Conflict between two family members consistently cools down once a third person gets pulled in, without ever actually being resolved between the original two
- Feeling that maintaining closeness with family requires either agreeing with them or cutting off contact, with little middle ground

## Why this matters for conflict

Bowen's framework is especially useful in family conflict because it explains why some disputes never seem to resolve, no matter how many conversations happen — the underlying pattern isn't really between the two people arguing, it's a triangle involving a third party who keeps the tension circulating rather than settled. Recognizing a triangle in progress ("I think I'm being pulled into something between them") can help someone step back from relaying messages or taking sides, and instead encourage the two original people to address each other directly.

Understanding differentiation offers a similarly practical shift: it reframes the goal during family conflict away from either "I have to agree to keep the peace" or "I have to cut off contact to protect myself," and toward a middle path — staying emotionally connected to family while still holding onto one's own perspective under pressure. This is valuable well beyond the family context; workplace dynamics often show the same triangulation pattern, such as two colleagues each complaining to a manager about the other instead of resolving the issue directly, or teams developing similar loyalty triangles under stress. Recognizing the pattern is often the first step toward interrupting it.
