# Betrayal and Trust Rebuilding (Perel-informed)

Source: Esther Perel (contemporary relational therapist and author, work spanning the 2010s–2020s, building on broader infidelity and trust-repair research)

Betrayal doesn't only mean infidelity, though that's the most commonly discussed form. At its core, betrayal is the experience of discovering that the story you believed about your relationship — what was promised, what was safe, what was true — wasn't accurate. It can come from an affair, but it can just as easily come from a hidden financial decision, a broken confidence shared with someone else, a family member siding against you in a dispute, or a colleague taking credit for shared work. What defines betrayal isn't the specific act but the shattering of an assumption the relationship was built on.

Perel's contribution to this conversation reframed how betrayal and recovery are typically understood. Rather than treating an affair or breach of trust purely as a moral failing to be judged, she encouraged looking at what the betrayal reveals about both people and the relationship's structure — without excusing the harm caused. She also emphasized that trust, once broken, isn't simply "rebuilt" back to its original form; it's rebuilt into something new, because both people now know something they didn't know before. The relationship that existed before the betrayal, in a sense, no longer exists — what comes after is a different relationship, built with fuller information.

Recovery, in this view, tends to move through recognizable phases: an initial period of crisis and disorientation, where the hurt person needs safety and honest answers more than solutions; a longer period of understanding, where both people try to make sense of how the betrayal happened, often uncovering issues in the relationship (or in one person's individual life) that predated it; and eventually a phase of deciding, consciously, what kind of relationship — if any — gets built going forward. Rushing this process, or skipping straight to "moving on," tends to leave the original wound unaddressed and liable to resurface.

## Recognizing the pattern

- The betrayed person repeatedly asks for the same details or reassurance, not because they didn't hear the answer, but because trust in the story itself hasn't been rebuilt yet
- Small, unrelated triggers (a phone left face-down, a late arrival, a vague explanation) provoke a reaction that seems disproportionate to the actual event
- The person who broke trust wants to move past the incident faster than the other person is ready to
- Conversations about the betrayal circle back to the same unresolved questions rather than progressing, because underlying safety hasn't been reestablished
- One or both people start comparing the current relationship to "before," as though trying to restore something rather than build something new

## Why this matters for conflict

In romantic breakups, family rifts, and even workplace disputes involving broken confidence or credit-taking, betrayal-related conflict often gets stuck because everyone is trying to solve it too quickly — apologize, forgive, move forward — without addressing that trust doesn't reset just because time has passed or an apology was offered. Recognizing that recovery happens in phases helps set more realistic expectations: repeated need for reassurance isn't a sign that someone is "stuck" or unwilling to forgive, and impatience from the person who caused the harm isn't necessarily a sign they don't care.

Understanding this pattern also shifts the goal away from "getting back to normal" and toward "figuring out what kind of relationship is possible now, with this new information." That reframe can be useful even in workplace HR conflicts — a team that discovers one member misrepresented shared work doesn't need to pretend nothing happened, but can consciously decide what working relationship is realistic going forward, built on clearer boundaries rather than a return to blind trust.
