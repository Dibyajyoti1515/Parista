# Rejection Sensitivity

Source: Downey & Feldman (1996)

Rejection sensitivity is the tendency to anxiously expect, readily perceive,
and intensely react to rejection from people who matter to us. People high
in rejection sensitivity often approach relationships already braced for
disappointment. Ambiguous behavior from a partner — a delayed reply, a
short message, a canceled plan — gets read as a deliberate signal of
rejection even when no rejection was intended.

This creates a self-fulfilling pattern. Because the anxious partner expects
rejection, they often react to perceived slights with hostility, withdrawal,
or excessive reassurance-seeking. These reactions can push the other person
away, which then confirms the original fear of rejection — even though the
initial "rejection" was never real to begin with.

Rejection sensitivity tends to develop from earlier experiences where
rejection was frequent, unpredictable, or came from someone whose approval
mattered deeply (a parent, an early romantic partner). It is not a fixed
trait; it can be triggered more strongly in some relationships than others,
and it tends to intensify during periods of relationship uncertainty or
conflict.

## Recognizing the pattern

Common signs someone may be experiencing rejection sensitivity in a
specific moment:
- Reading neutral or ambiguous behavior (a slow reply, a short text) as
  proof the other person is losing interest or is angry.
- A strong emotional reaction (anger, panic, sadness) that feels
  disproportionate to the actual event.
- An urge to seek immediate reassurance, or conversely, to withdraw first
  "before they can reject me."
- Difficulty believing reassurance even when it's given.

## Why this matters for conflict

In a breakup or a tense relationship moment, rejection sensitivity can turn
a small misunderstanding into a much larger rupture, because the anxious
partner's reaction to the perceived rejection often causes real friction —
independent of whatever the original ambiguous behavior actually meant.
Recognizing this pattern doesn't mean the person's feelings are invalid; it
means the *interpretation* of the triggering event may be worth examining
separately from the feeling itself.
