# Active Listening vs Reflexive Listening

Source: Carl Rogers and Richard Farson (active listening formalized in the 1950s, Rogers' client-centered therapy research), later expanded into conflict and communication training by Thomas Gordon (1970s)

Active listening, as described by psychologist Carl Rogers and later expanded by Richard Farson and Thomas Gordon, refers to a deliberate way of listening that prioritizes fully understanding what someone else is communicating — both the content and the feeling behind it — before responding. It typically involves giving full attention, reflecting back what was heard in one's own words to confirm understanding, and holding off on forming a response, defense, or counterpoint until the other person's point has been genuinely absorbed. Rogers developed this approach originally within therapy, based on the idea that people feel most understood, and are most able to actually hear feedback themselves, when they first experience being fully listened to without interruption or premature problem-solving.

What's often contrasted with this is a much more common, largely automatic pattern that can be called reflexive listening — listening primarily to formulate a response, rather than to understand. In this mode, attention is partly on the other person's words and partly on preparing a rebuttal, defense, or the next point to make, often before the other person has finished speaking. This kind of listening isn't necessarily intentional or disrespectful — it's frequently just a default habit, especially during conflict, when the instinct to defend a position or correct a misunderstanding tends to override the slower process of fully absorbing what's being said first.

The difference between the two isn't about being a better or worse person — it's a difference in what's actually happening cognitively while someone else is talking. Active listening requires temporarily setting aside the urge to respond, which takes conscious effort, especially under the stress of conflict when the instinct to defend oneself is strongest. Reflexive listening happens more automatically, and it often produces a subtly noticeable disconnect: the listener may technically hear all the words but respond to a slightly different point than what was actually said, because their attention had already partly shifted to their own reply.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Preparing a response or counterpoint while the other person is still talking, rather than focusing fully on what they're saying
- Responding to a slightly different point than what was actually raised, suggesting attention had shifted to formulating a reply
- Interrupting, or nearly interrupting, before someone has finished their thought during a tense conversation
- The other person repeating themselves or saying "that's not what I meant," suggesting their point wasn't fully absorbed the first time
- Feeling calmer and more willing to compromise after genuinely feeling heard, even before any actual solution has been proposed

## Why this matters for conflict

Reflexive listening is one of the most common, often invisible contributors to conflicts that feel like they're going in circles — both people are technically talking and responding, but neither fully absorbs what the other is saying, because both are partly focused on their own next point. This is especially common in heated romantic arguments, tense family conversations, and workplace disagreements, where the instinct to defend a position tends to override the slower, more effortful process of genuinely taking in what's being said.

Understanding this distinction offers a concrete, learnable shift: deliberately delaying a response long enough to reflect back what was heard — "it sounds like you're saying..." — before adding a counterpoint or defense. This isn't just a politeness technique; Rogers' research suggested that people who feel genuinely heard tend to become less defensive and more open to hearing the other side in return, which often does more to de-escalate conflict than any specific argument or solution offered. In practice, this means treating understanding the other person's point as a distinct, necessary first step — separate from and prior to responding to it — rather than assuming that hearing the words automatically means the point has been absorbed.
