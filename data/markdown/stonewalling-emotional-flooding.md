# Stonewalling and Emotional Flooding

Source: John Gottman (research from the 1990s onward, University of Washington "Love Lab" studies)

Emotional flooding describes what happens when conflict pushes someone's body past its ability to stay calm and think clearly. Heart rate spikes, breathing gets shallow, and the nervous system shifts into a fight-or-flight state — the same physiological response that would kick in during physical danger, even though the "threat" is really just a heated conversation. Once flooded, a person's ability to listen, reason, or problem-solve drops sharply, because the body is prioritizing survival over connection. This isn't a character flaw or a choice; it's a physical state that takes time to pass.

Stonewalling is one of the most common responses to flooding: shutting down, going quiet, avoiding eye contact, or physically leaving the conversation. From the outside, stonewalling often looks like indifference, stubbornness, or contempt — like the person has simply checked out and stopped caring about the conversation or the other person's feelings. But Gottman's research suggested that in many cases, stonewalling is actually a self-protective response to being overwhelmed, not a rejection of the other person. The stonewalling person often isn't thinking "I don't care" — they're often unable to access clear thought at all in that moment.

The trouble is that stonewalling and flooding tend to escalate each other. When one person shuts down, the other person — especially if they're already anxious about being heard — often pushes harder to get a response, which increases the flooded person's overwhelm and makes them shut down further. Both people end up caught in a cycle where the conversation becomes less and less productive the longer it continues, even though neither person intended that outcome.

## Recognizing the pattern

- Someone goes quiet, gives short one-word answers, or stops making eye contact partway through a disagreement
- Physical signs show up — a racing heart, shallow breathing, tension, or a flushed face — even in a "small" conflict
- A person tries to leave the room or end the conversation, and the other person follows or insists on continuing
- Conversations that go on for more than 20-30 minutes without a break tend to become repetitive or circular rather than productive
- After a flooded conversation, one or both people struggle to remember exactly what was said or agreed to

## Why this matters for conflict

In romantic relationships, family disputes, and even tense workplace meetings, flooding and stonewalling are often mistaken for the actual problem, when they're really a sign that the conversation needs to pause. Pushing to "finish the conversation" while someone is flooded rarely produces a real resolution — it usually produces words said in frustration, promises that don't stick, or a shutdown that leaves the original issue unresolved. Recognizing the physical signs of flooding, in oneself or someone else, reframes stonewalling from "they don't care" to "their nervous system needs a break before this conversation can go anywhere useful."

This understanding supports a very practical shift: agreeing, ideally before conflict happens, that either person can call a pause when flooding sets in — with a genuine commitment to return to the conversation once things calm down, typically after twenty minutes or more, since that's roughly how long it takes a flooded body to physiologically settle. In workplace settings, this might look like tabling a heated discussion until a follow-up meeting rather than pushing through in the moment. The goal isn't to avoid the conflict, but to have the conversation when both people are actually capable of hearing each other.
