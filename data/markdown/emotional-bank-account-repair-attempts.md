# Emotional Bank Account / Repair Attempts

Source: John Gottman (research from the 1990s onward, University of Washington "Love Lab" studies)

Think of every relationship as holding a kind of emotional account. Small moments of connection — a kind word, a shared laugh, checking in about someone's rough day, even just making eye contact and smiling — work like deposits. Small moments of disconnection, dismissal, or criticism work like withdrawals. Over time, the balance in that account shapes how much goodwill and patience two people have for each other, especially when conflict shows up.

This idea explains something a lot of people notice but can't quite name: the same disagreement can feel minor in one relationship and explosive in another. The difference often isn't the disagreement itself — it's the balance sitting in the account beforehand. A couple, a parent and adult child, or two coworkers with a strong reserve of goodwill can have a tense exchange and recover quickly. The same exchange in a relationship with a depleted account can spiral, because there's little stored trust to cushion the impact.

Closely tied to this is the idea of a "repair attempt" — any small effort, verbal or nonverbal, to de-escalate tension or reconnect during or after a conflict. A repair attempt might be as simple as a half-joke to break tension, a hand reaching out, an apology, or even just softening one's tone mid-argument. What matters most isn't how smooth or eloquent the repair attempt is — it's whether the other person notices it and lets it land. In relationships with a healthy emotional balance, repair attempts tend to be recognized and accepted easily. In relationships running low, they're more likely to be missed, dismissed, or even rejected outright.

## Recognizing the pattern

- One or both people seem to interpret neutral comments as criticism, as if there's little benefit of the doubt left to give
- Small efforts to lighten the mood during an argument (a joke, a touch, a change in tone) get ignored or brushed off rather than acknowledged
- Everyday moments of connection — greetings, check-ins, small favors — have become rare or feel effortful rather than automatic
- Arguments seem to escalate quickly from a small trigger, as if there's little cushion to absorb friction
- After a conflict, one person tries to reconnect (an apology, a text, a peace offering) and the other doesn't respond or downplays it

## Why this matters for conflict

Understanding the emotional bank account reframes conflict itself. Instead of asking "was this argument handled well or badly," it becomes useful to ask "what's the balance been like lately, and did anyone try to repair during this?" A breakup, a family rift, or a workplace blowup often isn't caused by one bad conversation — it's the result of withdrawals accumulating faster than deposits for a long stretch, until even a small trigger has little goodwill to draw on.

This is genuinely useful in practice. It suggests that the way to prevent future blowups isn't only about avoiding conflict or having better arguments — it's about consistently making small deposits between conflicts, so there's a reserve to draw from when tension does arise. It also highlights the importance of noticing and accepting repair attempts, even clumsy ones. Someone who recognizes a partner's awkward joke or a coworker's brief "hey, sorry about earlier" as a genuine repair attempt — rather than dismissing it — gives the relationship a chance to reset before the balance drops further. Conversely, someone who realizes they've been ignoring repair attempts, or not offering any themselves, can use that awareness to shift the pattern, without needing to relitigate every past disagreement first.
