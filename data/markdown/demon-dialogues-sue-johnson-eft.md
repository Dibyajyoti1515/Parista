# Demon Dialogues (Sue Johnson, EFT)

Source: Sue Johnson (developed through Emotionally Focused Therapy research, 1980s–2000s)

Demon dialogues describe the predictable, self-reinforcing arguments that couples and close relationships fall into over and over, often about seemingly small things, that always seem to end in the same painful place. Sue Johnson, who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, observed that beneath the surface content of these fights — who forgot to do the dishes, who was late, who didn't call back — is usually a deeper fear about the relationship itself: am I safe here, do you still care, can I count on you. The argument on the surface is rarely what the fight is actually about.

Johnson described a few recurring patterns these dialogues tend to take. The most common is "Find the Bad Guy," where both people are trying to prove the other is at fault, each escalating in self-defense. Another is the "Protest Polka," often called pursue-withdraw: one person pushes for connection or answers, getting louder or more insistent as they feel unheard, while the other pulls back, shuts down, or goes quiet to avoid conflict — which then makes the first person push even harder, feeling more abandoned, which makes the second person withdraw even further. A third is "Freeze and Flee," where both people disengage entirely, leaving the relationship feeling emotionally flat rather than actively conflictual.

What makes these dialogues so sticky is that each person's behavior, which feels like a reasonable reaction to the other's behavior, is actually what triggers the very response they're afraid of. The pursuer's insistence pushes the withdrawer further away; the withdrawer's silence convinces the pursuer they're being abandoned. Both people end up reinforcing the exact fear driving the other person's behavior, without either one intending to.

## Recognizing the pattern

- The same argument seems to repeat in different disguises — different topics, same emotional shape and same ending
- One person tends to raise their voice, ask repeated questions, or push for a response, while the other goes quiet, changes the subject, or leaves the room
- Both people walk away from conflicts feeling like the other one is the problem, with little sense of shared vulnerability underneath
- Small triggers (a tone of voice, a delayed reply, a missed check-in) spark disproportionately intense reactions
- After the argument, both people feel more distant or less safe with each other, even if the original issue gets "resolved"

## Why this matters for conflict

Recognizing a demon dialogue changes what the conflict is actually about. Instead of relitigating who's right about the dishes or the late text, it becomes possible to notice the underlying cycle: one person reaching for connection in a way that reads as attack, and the other protecting themselves in a way that reads as rejection. This reframe is especially useful in romantic relationships and close family bonds, where the same fight can resurface for years without resolution, because the surface topic keeps changing while the underlying cycle stays the same.

Understanding this pattern helps someone step outside the cycle rather than just playing their assigned role in it. A person prone to pursuing can learn to name the fear underneath their insistence ("I push because I'm scared you're pulling away") rather than escalating. A person prone to withdrawing can learn to signal they're overwhelmed rather than going silent, which often reads as indifference. Naming the cycle out loud — "I think we're doing the thing again" — can interrupt it before it runs its usual course, because both people start responding to the actual fear instead of to each other's defensive behavior.
