# Adult Attachment Theory

Source: Fraley (2018), building on foundational attachment research

Attachment theory describes how early bonds with caregivers shape the way
people relate to close others throughout life, including in adult romantic
relationships. Research typically describes attachment along two
dimensions: anxiety (fear of abandonment, need for closeness and
reassurance) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness, preference for
independence). Combinations of these two dimensions produce broad patterns
often described as secure, anxious, avoidant, and anxious-avoidant
(sometimes called "disorganized" or "fearful-avoidant").

## Secure attachment

Comfortable with closeness and comfortable with independence. Tends to
communicate needs directly, trust a partner's intentions, and recover from
conflict without lasting anxiety about the relationship's stability.

## Anxious attachment

High need for closeness and reassurance, often paired with a persistent
fear that a partner will leave or lose interest. May seek frequent
reassurance, feel destabilized by a partner's need for space, or interpret
distance as a sign of impending loss.

## Avoidant attachment

Values independence highly and can feel overwhelmed or "crowded" by a
partner's emotional needs. May withdraw during conflict or intense
emotional moments, not necessarily because they don't care, but because
closeness itself can feel threatening to a sense of self-sufficiency.

## Why this matters for conflict

A common and difficult dynamic is when a more anxiously-attached partner
pursues closeness during conflict (wanting to talk it through immediately)
while a more avoidantly-attached partner withdraws to self-regulate — each
person's coping response can intensify the other's fear. The anxious
partner's pursuit can feel like pressure to the avoidant partner, and the
avoidant partner's withdrawal can feel like confirmation of abandonment to
the anxious partner. Neither response is wrong on its own; the mismatch in
timing and need is often the actual source of escalation, not the original
disagreement.

Attachment patterns are not fixed diagnoses — they describe tendencies that
can shift across relationships and with awareness and effort.
